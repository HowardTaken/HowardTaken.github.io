About

I'm Haoran Ding, and most people call me Howard. I'm an Electrical Engineering student at the UCLA Samueli School of Engineering, graduating in 2029.

How I got into engineering

I got into engineering through underwater robotics. In high school I served as CEO of a MATE ROV team for three seasons, leading a multidisciplinary group through the full design, build, and pilot cycle of a remotely operated vehicle. In 2023 we won 1st Place Overall and 1st Place in Engineering & Communication at the MATE ROV Monterey Regional. The biggest lesson from those seasons was about integration: individual subsystems rarely fail on their own. Before starting at UCLA, I also spent a summer as a student researcher at UC Santa Barbara, designing bio-inspired snake-scale prototypes to reduce friction. That was my first experience with a real research loop: build a prototype, measure it, find out why it failed, and redesign it. It also taught me to quantify results with MATLAB and motion tracking instead of judging them by eye.

Photonics and the measurement bench

At UCLA I've moved toward photonics and semiconductor devices. As a SURP Research Fellow in the Terahertz and Infrared Photonics Group, I work with terahertz quantum cascade lasers. They are among the few compact, solid-state sources in a part of the spectrum that is otherwise hard to reach. My main project was a device that had never been measured: a metal–metal waveguide THz QCL. I brought it up, characterized it across bias and temperature, and established its operating envelope: a 2.45–2.61 THz tuning range and a maximum operating temperature of 133 K.

Most of the engineering effort went into the software around the bench. The lab's test setup had been configured by hand through LabVIEW, which made measurements slow and hard to reproduce exactly. I built a MATLAB automation suite that communicates with the step-scan FTIR spectrometer, function generator, and lock-in amplifier over GPIB and runs full characterization sequences on its own. I paired it with analysis scripts that extract key device parameters and a GUI so other lab members can run standardized tests without editing code. All of it is maintained under Git.

That combination is what I enjoy most: a physical device on the bench and the software that makes sense of it. A measurement is only as trustworthy as the pipeline behind it, and I prefer to own both the hardware and the software.

Bringing up a terahertz quantum cascade laser, and automating the bench that measures it

I independently characterized a previously untested metal–metal waveguide THz quantum cascade laser. Along the way, I replaced the lab's manual test configuration with an automated MATLAB measurement and analysis pipeline.

Role

Tools
MATLAB, GPIB, step-scan FTIR, lock-in amplifier, function generator, cryostat, Git
Outcome
Full operating envelope of a new device, plus a reusable test suite for the group

Bias tuning range
133 K

Background

The terahertz band sits between microwaves and the infrared. It is useful for spectroscopy, imaging, and sensing, but it has historically been hard to generate with compact sources. Quantum cascade lasers (QCLs) are one of the few solid-state options. Unlike a conventional diode laser, a QCL emits light as electrons cascade through a carefully engineered stack of quantum wells, so the emission frequency is set by the layer design rather than by the material's bandgap.

The device I worked on uses a metal–metal waveguide, in which the active region is sandwiched between two metal layers. This geometry confines the optical mode tightly, which helps the laser keep working at higher temperatures. The tradeoff is that light couples out of the device less efficiently, so measurements are sensitive and benefit from careful, repeatable technique.

The problem

The lab's characterization workflow relied on configuring instruments manually through LabVIEW. Every change in operating condition, such as a new bias point, a switch between pulsed and continuous-wave drive, or a new temperature, meant reconfiguring the setup by hand. That made full characterization slow, made it hard to repeat a measurement exactly, and created opportunities for human error between runs.

What I built

I developed a MATLAB test automation suite that communicates with each instrument over GPIB and sequences the whole multi-instrument setup from a single program:

  1. Configure
    Lab members set test parameters in a GUI, with no code edits required.
  2. Control
    MATLAB drives the function generator, lock-in amplifier, and FTIR over GPIB.
  3. Bias the DUT
    The laser is swept across bias in pulsed and continuous-wave modes.
  4. Acquire
    Optical output and spectra are captured automatically at each operating point.
  5. Analyze
    Scripts generate performance plots and extract device parameters.

The automated characterization pipeline, from test configuration to extracted parameters.

  • Instrument control. Scripts handle communication with the step-scan FTIR spectrometer, function generator, and lock-in amplifier, replacing manual setup in LabVIEW.
  • Characterization sequences. Full test runs bias the device under test and capture its output across pulsed and continuous-wave operating conditions with no manual reconfiguration between steps, which makes results directly repeatable.
  • Data analysis. Analysis scripts process the acquired data, generate performance plots, and extract the parameters that define the device's behavior, so they can be compared with expected operating behavior.
  • GUI for the group. A MATLAB GUI lets other lab members configure parameters and run standardized validation sequences without touching the underlying code. The project is maintained under Git so procedures stay documented and reproducible across the team.

Parameters extracted

Threshold current density
The current density at which the device begins to lase. It is the main figure of merit for how efficiently the active region reaches optical gain.
Slope efficiency
How much additional optical power the laser produces per unit of additional drive current above threshold.
Characteristic temperature
How quickly the threshold rises as the device heats up. It summarizes the laser's temperature sensitivity and ultimately limits its maximum operating temperature.

Results

Using this pipeline, I brought up the previously untested device and established its full operating envelope. The laser tunes from 2.45 to 2.61 THz with bias and operates up to a maximum temperature of 133 K. The automation suite and GUI remain in use, giving the group a standardized, repeatable way to characterize future devices.

Quantum RAG Assistant

A retrieval-augmented assistant for semiconductor physics literature that answers questions with cited sources. It started as a tool for my own lab reading and grew into a full-stack web application.

Stack
Python, ChromaDB, LangGraph, React, TypeScript
Focus
Grounded answers, agentic self-critique, production-style backend
Source
github.com/HowardTaken/quantum-rag-assistant

Motivation

Research in my lab draws on dense semiconductor physics literature, and finding the right passage across many papers is slow. Keyword search works poorly here because the same idea is often described in different terms. I wanted a tool that could answer a specific question using the papers themselves and show exactly where each answer came from.

How it works

  • Ingestion. Papers are parsed, split into chunks, embedded, and stored in a ChromaDB vector database, which enables semantic search by meaning rather than exact wording.
  • Agentic retrieval. The assistant is implemented as a LangGraph agent. It retrieves relevant passages, drafts an answer, and then runs a self-critique step that checks the draft against the retrieved evidence. If the answer is not well supported, the agent loops back and retries before responding.
  • Citations. Every answer includes its sources, so claims can be checked against the original papers. For research use, that traceability is essential.

From script to web application

Once the core pipeline proved useful, I turned it into a full-stack application:

  • A React/TypeScript frontend, deployed through a CI/CD pipeline.
  • Production-style backend safeguards, including authentication, rate limiting, and CORS configuration.
  • Persistent multi-turn conversation state, so follow-up questions build on earlier context instead of starting over.

Takeaway

The project took me from a working prototype to a deployable service. Retrieval quality and the self-critique loop determine whether answers are trustworthy, while the surrounding infrastructure (auth, rate limits, state, deployment) determines whether anyone else can actually use it.

Project · 2026

AI-Powered Market Analyst Agent

A multi-source financial intelligence pipeline that turns raw company data into a structured investment memo covering valuation and risk, cutting research time from hours to seconds.

Stack
Python, Gemini API, Financial Modeling Prep API
Focus
Data integration, domain-aware edge cases, structured generation
Source
github.com/HowardTaken/AI-Financial-Analyst

The problem

A first-pass investment memo means pulling financial statements, computing valuation metrics, reading filings, and writing up risks. That can easily take hours of manual work. I wanted to automate the whole path from raw data to a structured, readable analysis.

Approach

  • Multi-source pipeline. The Python pipeline combines structured financial data from the Financial Modeling Prep API with unstructured regulatory filings, assembling both into a single context for the Gemini model.
  • End-to-end generation. The model produces a structured memo with valuation and risk analysis, so output is consistent from company to company.
  • Domain-aware edge cases. The pipeline correctly excludes banking institutions from discounted cash flow (DCF) valuation. For a bank, debt and deposits are part of normal operations rather than financing, so the standard free-cash-flow model does not produce a meaningful value. Detecting this case instead of blindly applying a DCF keeps the output from being confidently wrong.

Result

Generating an investment memo went from hours of manual research to seconds. The more important lesson was that useful automation depends on encoding domain rules explicitly, not just passing data to a model.

Project · 2026

Autonomous Line-Following Robot

A closed-loop PID controller on an Arduino that integrates an IR sensor array with motor driver circuitry to track a line smoothly and accurately.

Stack
C++, Arduino, IR sensor array, motor driver
Focus
Real-time feedback control and empirical gain tuning
Source
github.com/HowardTaken/ece3-pid-line-follower

How it works

An IR sensor array under the robot measures where the line sits relative to the center of the chassis. On every control cycle, that reading becomes a position error, and a PID controller converts the error into a steering correction applied through the motor driver:

  • Proportional action steers back toward the line in proportion to how far off it is.
  • Integral action removes persistent offset, so the robot doesn't settle slightly to one side.
  • Derivative action damps the response, so the robot doesn't overshoot and oscillate.

Tuning

I tuned the gain coefficients empirically to minimize tracking error and oscillation. The hardest condition was variable-contrast surfaces: when the difference between line and background changes, the sensor readings shift, and gains that work well on one surface can cause oscillation on another. The final tuning balanced responsiveness against stability across those conditions.

Bruin Underwater Robotics: autonomous underwater vehicle

As a Robotics Engineer on UCLA's ASME underwater robotics team, I contribute to the mechanical design of an autonomous underwater vehicle (AUV).

Role
Robotics Engineer, mechanical design team
Areas
Chassis frame architecture, thruster configuration
Collaboration
Electrical subsystem team, component integration

I contribute to the AUV's chassis frame architecture and thruster configuration. The frame and thruster layout determine how the vehicle moves and holds its position underwater, so the work involves trade-offs between several competing goals:

  • Rigidity. The frame has to stay stiff enough to keep thrusters and sensors aligned without adding unnecessary mass.
  • Buoyancy distribution. How buoyancy and weight are distributed affects the vehicle's resting attitude and how much work the thrusters must do to hold position.
  • Hydrodynamic stability. Thruster placement and orientation determine how predictably the vehicle can be controlled in each direction of motion.

Because an AUV has to navigate on its own, mechanical choices directly affect how hard the control problem becomes. I work closely with the electrical subsystem team on component integration so that electronics, wiring, and enclosures fit the frame without compromising those goals.

Research · UC Santa Barbara · June – July 2024

Bio-inspired snake-scale surfaces for friction reduction

As a student researcher, I engineered prototypes inspired by snake scales to reduce tribological friction, and used quantitative motion analysis to guide each design iteration.

Role
Student Researcher
Tools
CAD, 3D printing, MATLAB, motion-tracking software
Focus
Design for manufacturability, quantitative testing

Snake scales are a well-known example of a natural surface that controls friction through geometry. The project aimed to reproduce that effect in engineered, interlocking scale prototypes.

  • Iterating on manufacturability. Early designs ran into critical 3D-printing tolerance failures, where interlocking features came out too tight or too loose to work as intended. I redesigned the interlocking mechanisms iteratively until the parts printed and assembled reliably.
  • Measuring instead of guessing. I evaluated each prototype with motion-tracking software and processed the resulting displacement and velocity datasets in MATLAB. Comparing designs quantitatively shortened each design iteration cycle.
